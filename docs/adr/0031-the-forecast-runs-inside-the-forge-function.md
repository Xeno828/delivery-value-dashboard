# 0031 — The forecast runs inside the Forge function, and the calculator is retired

[ADR 0008](0008-forge-calls-a-hosted-calculator.md) chose a hosted calculator because Forge could not run `agent/tools/` and the alternative was a second implementation of the forecast. Its amendment of 2026-09-01 records that a third shape exists and measures it: the same Python, unchanged, under Pyodide — CPython compiled to WebAssembly — inside the Forge function, every figure byte-identical to native, nothing leaving Atlassian. That amendment left the choice to the person accountable for it.

**Decided 2026-09-01: the app takes that shape.** The forecast, the facts and the sequencing run inside the Forge function; the hosted calculator is retired; the `remotes` block leaves the manifest; the app becomes eligible for the *Runs on Atlassian* badge and is `PINNED` for data residency by construction. The settled design was reached in four rounds of questions, each answered against a measurement or a documented limit rather than a preference, and the rounds are what this record preserves. The first three were answered from the first probe and from Atlassian's documentation; the fourth corrected three of their numbers against a second probe built for the purpose. The evidence is `docs/research/2026-09-01-runs-on-atlassian-badge.md` §6, `docs/research/2026-09-01-forge-async-events.md` and `docs/research/2026-09-02-second-probe-consumer-and-snapshot.md`.

The property this trades on is the one every record here rests on: **one implementation of every figure.** The Python moves; nothing is rewritten. What it costs is a forecast about ten times slower on Forge's CPU, a seven-second cold load per container, and one route that no longer fits a synchronous call.

## Sequencing is asynchronous, and only sequencing

> **Corrected 2026-09-03: the forecast is asynchronous too.** The paragraph below and the sentence *"facts and the forecast stay synchronous"* rested on the second probe's forecast timings, which were taken over a dense synthetic board where every trial completes in a few dozen draws. On the dev site's own board — 906 working-day observations, most of them zero, 31 open items — `forecast_completion` walks about 280 days per trial before the open items complete, some five million draws across 20,000 trials, and `/v1/forecast-context` took **12 to 15 seconds** inside the resolver function under the snapshot, measured on three deploys (8.4.0 to 8.6.0), with memory and the Node version ruled out by deploy and the same call reproduced at 1.3 s natively. With four seconds of Jira reads around it the invocation reached 17 to 22 seconds against the adapter's fifteen and the function's hard twenty-five. So the forecast takes the shape sequencing took, on the same machinery: the resolver chooses the slice synchronously, which is cheap, then pushes; the consumer computes on the plain runtime; `jobResult` finishes the answer — the at-risk items' titles re-read from Jira by key as the collector, the calibration log written if it moved — and the adapter polls both routes. Facts, history and the burndown are bounded and stay synchronous. One consequence carried into the design: a forecast's payload is a team's whole history, past the event's 100 KB limit on any real board, so the projection travels through app storage in chunks under the 240 KiB row limit, written by the resolver before the push and deleted by the consumer as it reads them, one path for both kinds. The reader waits about ten seconds warm and twenty cold where the calculator answered in five; the tile says it is running 20,000 simulations while it does. *Rejected* below gains nothing: polling stays in the adapter, and no result is cached.


The bridge adapter gives every call fifteen seconds. Sequencing is **cubic in the ask count** — natively 4 asks take 1.1 s, 8 take 7 s, 12 take 21 s and 16 take 48 s — and Forge's CPU runs the plain runtime at ten times native throughout: 11 s, 68 s, 209 s and 479 s. The service's old cap of fifty asks was never reachable on any transport; it is about twenty-five minutes natively. No synchronous resolver answers even four asks after a cold start, and today the page's *"No sequencing for this selection"* is what that already produces on the dev site.

So the `sequence` resolver validates, projects, and pushes one async event carrying the projection; a consumer function runs the tools with a 900-second class budget; the result waits in app storage; and **the adapter polls and hands the page one final `{ok, status, body}`**. The page is untouched and so is [ADR 0009](0009-one-contract-two-transports.md)'s contract, because the adapter is the one script permitted to know what Forge is, and the body shapes it returns are the ones `serve_live.py` already produces synchronously. The file transport does not change at all.

Facts and the forecast stay synchronous. Measured warm on Forge, the forecast is 1.5 s at 480 issues and 2.8 s at 4,800, and facts 0.3 s and 2.5 s; board size barely moves them. Cold, they ride on the resolver function's snapshot load below and answer in about 6 s at the large board, inside the adapter's clock.

## The consumer's contract

- **Cap: twelve asks, everywhere.** The cap lives in the tool, so the file transport refuses at the same number in the same sentence and the page prints one refusal whichever transport it has. Twelve asks is 209 s on Forge and 21 s natively; sixteen would fit the budget below with a 1.9× margin and was declined because eight minutes is longer than a reader should watch a tile. The old fifty was a number nobody had run.
- **Budget: 900 seconds and 1,024 MB**, declared on the consumer function. The ceiling rather than the need, because Forge bills measured time on a run that finishes, and a slower container than the probe's is the one unknown left. The adapter's own ceiling is ten minutes.
- **One guard against a run that cannot finish.** With the cost shape known, the count cap is the guard, and it is deterministic; the timing estimate first proposed for it assumed a linear cost and is dropped. What stays is the retry guard: on entry the consumer reads Forge's retry context, and on any retry it writes a refusal to the job row and returns without computing. Measured, a consumer killed at its timeout is re-invoked forty seconds later with `retryReason: FUNCTION_TIME_OUT`, and again forty seconds after the next kill, for a day; a consumer without the guard runs the same doomed job every six minutes for something nobody sees.
- **The event carries the projection.** A consumer cannot read Jira as the viewer, so the resolver projects as it does today and passes the result on. A call is about 16 KB and does not grow with the customer; the documented limit is 100 KB per event once the function's timeout exceeds 55 s, and the resolver refuses with a sentence above it rather than truncating. A storage detour for the payload earns its place only if that refusal ever fires.
- **A reload joins the job in flight.** A reader who reloads the page mid-job would otherwise start a second three-minute computation. The resolver keys in-flight jobs by context, dataset content and asking account, and a second request for the same key collects the running job rather than pushing another. That is not the cached result rejected below: it is one computation with two collectors, and the row is still deleted on collection.
- **The job row.** Key-value storage under an unguessable job key, a status the consumer moves through started, done and refused, a timestamp, and the asking account. The poll resolver refuses any other account, so [ADR 0018](0018-permission-mirroring-holds-by-accident-and-where-it-does-not.md)'s mirroring holds by a comparison rather than by entropy. The row is deleted on collection and carries a one-hour TTL; the poll checks the row's own timestamp, because an expired row may still be readable for 48 hours. Forge's job-stats API is not used: it carries counts, no payload and no documented lifetime, and the row must exist to hold the result regardless.

## How the runtime travels

Forge's bundler ships only compiled JavaScript and rewrites Pyodide's own dynamic import into something that fails. The runtime's five files and the Python sources therefore travel as base64 inside a generated module, unpacked to `/tmp` on first invocation and loaded from there; a warm container keeps the loaded runtime, and the consumer runs in its own container, concurrently with other jobs unless told otherwise.

**The resolver function loads from a memory snapshot; the consumer does not.** A snapshot taken after the sources are imported cuts Forge's cold start from 11 s to 1.3 s, and on Forge's runtime — not locally — everything computed afterwards runs 1.65× slower, measured A/B in one container. The resolver function answers synchronously under the adapter's clock, where the cold start is the risk; the consumer runs minutes of computation, where throughput is. Same generated bundle, one flag per function. The snapshot is made at deploy on the developer's machine and loads on Forge's Linux runtime with identical figures; both Pyodide options are undocumented, which is a dependency this record accepts with its eyes open. That module is **generated at deploy, never committed**: `make forge-deploy` produces it from the pinned `pyodide` npm package (314.0.6 at the time of writing) and from the Python read straight out of `agent/tools/` and `service/`. A committed copy of the Python is a second copy, and a second copy drifts.

The pure half of `service/app.py` — the projection, the caps, the refusals — moves into its own module that both the hosted service and the function import. The HTTP server and the two auth verifiers stay behind in `app.py` and go when the calculator does.

## How it is proven, and in what order

A sixth suite runs the tools under Pyodide in local Node and asserts the answer equals native Python byte for byte over the fixtures `tests/test_service.py` already uses. CI runs it on every push. That assertion is the successor of the hosted service's parity test and the only thing standing between two runtimes and a figure that drifts between them unnoticed.

Before the real app was touched, a second throwaway app on the dev site settled what documentation could not, by deploying. Adding a consumer module and a second function is a **minor** version, and so is raising a function's timeout; adding a remote is **major**, with the linter naming *"data residency or egress modification"*; removing that remote is **minor**. The service's validation layer imports under Pyodide unchanged. The forecast at a large board fits. And the snapshot does what the paragraph above says. None of it is a hedge any more.

Then routes move **one per commit**, deployed to development and clicked on the dev site before the next, with the calculator answering everything not yet moved. There is no runtime switch: git is the switch, each deploy has one change to blame, and a switch that outlives the migration is a second code path nobody tests. The `remotes` block is removed in its own final commit — a minor version by the probe's deploy, and **a major version when it came to it on 2026-09-03**: the probe's remote was a bare `baseUrl`, this app's declared realms and `operations: [compute]`, and the platform classifies removing that as a data-residency modification in both directions. Approved with the only installation still the dev site. Until then the badge is not earned, and [ADR 0012](0012-the-calculator-is-reached-by-invokeremote.md) and [ADR 0030](0030-the-manifest-commits-to-its-hostnames-and-realms-once.md) stay true; at that commit they are marked superseded by this one, and the calculator's three services, three registries, region loop and realm guard go with them.

## Rejected

**A hard refusal at two or three asks, keeping the resolver synchronous.** Cheapest to build, and it sells a sequencing tile that refuses the case it exists for while the file transport keeps fifty.

**Pyodide's snapshot on the consumer.** It shortens the cold load and slows every second of computation after it by two thirds on Forge. On a function that runs for minutes that is the wrong side of the trade.

**Pyodide in the Custom UI iframe.** Needs `unsafe-eval` in the content security policy, a major version; runs at less than half speed in a hidden tab; and the weekly brief has no browser to run in.

**Polling in the page.** It would teach `src/app.js` what a job is, which is a transport detail, and the single file would carry it to every reader who never has one.

**Caching a result by board and dataset.** A figure served from storage without saying when it was computed is the quiet wrongness [ADR 0017](0017-a-forecast-is-logged-as-a-count-not-a-promise.md) exists to stop, and the seed already makes a recompute identical.

**Committing the generated runtime module.** Eighteen megabytes in git, and a second copy of the Python.

**Moving every route in one deploy.** One change to blame per deploy is worth more than one fewer deploy.

**A first-ask timing estimate as the budget guard.** It priced sequencing per ask. The cost is cubic, and a count is both honest and deterministic.

**Sixteen asks.** It fits, and it is eight minutes of a reader's attention for a comparison; twelve is three and a half.

Not decided here: when this lands relative to the first external trial. The whole route is minor versions by the documentation, so it can land on either side of that date without a reinstall; the probe's deploy says whether that holds.
